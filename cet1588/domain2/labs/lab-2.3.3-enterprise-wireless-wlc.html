One brain for every access point.

In the last two labs every access point was autonomous — a standalone device you configured by hand, one at a time. That's fine for two APs. But the CBT Miami office is growing to dozens of APs across multiple floors, and configuring each individually (and keeping them all consistent) becomes impossible. The enterprise answer is a Wireless LAN Controller (WLC): a single appliance that manages many lightweight access points centrally. You configure an SSID once on the controller and it pushes to every AP at once. In this lab you'll deploy a WLC, register two lightweight APs to it, broadcast one SSID across both, and watch a client roam seamlessly between them.

Two AP architectures.

The single most important wireless-architecture concept on the Network+ exam is the distinction between autonomous and lightweight access points. An autonomous AP is self-contained — it holds its own configuration and makes its own decisions. A lightweight AP is "dumb" on purpose: it holds almost no configuration of its own and relies on a central Wireless LAN Controller for everything. Which one you choose determines how the whole wireless network is managed.

Network+ Context — Read this first

The Network+ N10-009 exam is vendor-neutral. You will not type Cisco commands on test day. Packet Tracer is used here because it can actually run a wireless LAN controller, register lightweight APs to it, and let a client roam between them — making an abstract architecture tangible. The exam-tested concepts — autonomous vs lightweight APs, the role of a WLC, centralized management, and roaming — are vendor-neutral. Focus on what the controller does and why a large network needs one.

CAPWAP — how a lightweight AP talks to its controller

A lightweight AP discovers and connects to the WLC using CAPWAP (Control And Provisioning of Wireless Access Points). Once joined, the AP downloads its configuration from the controller and tunnels client traffic and management back to it. This is sometimes called a split-MAC architecture: time-sensitive radio functions stay on the AP, while higher-level decisions (authentication, policy, roaming) are handled centrally by the WLC.

What the controller centralizes
Configuration
Push once, apply everywhere

Create an SSID, security policy, or VLAN mapping once on the WLC and it deploys to every registered AP. No more touching each AP by hand.

RF management
Auto channel & power

The controller can automatically assign non-overlapping channels and adjust transmit power across all APs so they don't interfere — the channel-planning work you'd otherwise do by hand.

Roaming & security
Seamless handoff

Because all APs share one controller and one SSID (ESSID), a client moving through the building hands off between APs without re-authenticating or dropping the connection.

Roaming and the ESSID

Recall the ESSID from Lab 2.3.1: the same SSID shared across multiple APs so they form one logical network. That's exactly what makes roaming possible. Both LAP-1 and LAP-2 broadcast CBT-Corp. A laptop carried from one end of the building to the other associates to whichever AP has the stronger signal, handing off as it moves — all under one network name, with no manual reconnection. The WLC coordinates the handoff so the client keeps its session.

Add the Wireless LAN Controller

Drag a Wireless LAN Controller onto the canvas

From Network Devices → Wireless Devices, drag a WLC (e.g., the WLC-3504). Rename it WLC. Cable a switch access port in VLAN 10 to one of the WLC's ports (the controller's management/data port).

Give the WLC its management IP

You'll set the management interface to 192.168.10.240 / 255.255.255.0, gateway 192.168.10.1, in the Configure tab. A controller must have a stable static address that APs and admins can always reach.

Add the lightweight APs

Drag two Lightweight Access Points

From Wireless Devices, drag two LAP (lightweight access point) devices — not the standard autonomous AP. Rename them LAP-1 and LAP-2. Place them apart from each other so a client can roam between their coverage areas.

Cable each LAP to the switch in VLAN 10

Connect each LAP's wired port to a switch access port in VLAN 10 (e.g., Fa0/8 and Fa0/9). The lightweight APs will pull DHCP addresses and then automatically discover and register to the WLC.

Add a roaming client

Drag a Laptop with a wireless module (or a smartphone). Rename it ROAM-LAPTOP. Position it between the two APs so you can drag it back and forth to demonstrate roaming.

Lightweight vs autonomous — pick the right device

Packet Tracer's wireless tray has both autonomous access points (used in 2.3.1/2.3.2) and lightweight APs. A lightweight AP will not function on its own — it must register to a WLC. If you accidentally drop an autonomous AP, it won't appear in the controller's AP list no matter what you do. Confirm you've placed lightweight APs for this lab.

With the WLC and two LAPs cabled in, move to the Configure tab.

The "aha" moment — you never configured the APs

Stop and notice what just happened: you set up the SSID exactly once, on the controller. Both LAP-1 and LAP-2 are broadcasting it, yet you never opened either AP's config. Now imagine 50 APs instead of 2 — the controller model is the only way that scales. To change the passphrase across the whole building, you edit one WLAN on the WLC, not 50 access points.

Simulator note — WLC registration timing

In Packet Tracer, lightweight APs can take a short while (and sometimes a "fast-forward time" click) to complete CAPWAP discovery and register to the controller. If an AP doesn't appear in the WLC's AP list immediately, give it a moment, click fast-forward time, and confirm the AP has a DHCP address and can reach the WLC's management IP. Real controllers behave the same way — APs cycle through discovery → join → image/config download before going live.

What "good" looks like
The registration chain — diagnose in order

A lightweight AP must complete a chain before it can serve clients: (1) get a DHCP IP → (2) discover the WLC's management IP → (3) join the controller via CAPWAP → (4) download config → (5) broadcast the WLAN. If an AP isn't broadcasting, find which step broke: no IP = DHCP/VLAN problem; IP but not joined = can't reach the WLC; joined but no SSID = WLAN not enabled or not mapped to the AP. Walking the chain in order is exactly how you'd troubleshoot real controller-based wireless.

When the AP won't join.

Seven failure modes. Most controller problems are a lightweight AP that can't reach or join the WLC.

A lightweight AP never appears in the WLC's AP list
Cause
The AP can't reach the controller — no DHCP address, wrong VLAN, or it can't resolve/reach the WLC management IP.
Diagnose
Confirm the AP got a 192.168.10.x address. From a host in that VLAN, ping the WLC (192.168.10.240). Click fast-forward time to let discovery finish.
Fix
Place the AP's switch port in VLAN 10, ensure DHCP works, and confirm the WLC management IP is reachable.
You dropped an AP but it acts completely standalone and ignores the WLC
Cause
You placed an autonomous AP, not a lightweight one. Autonomous APs don't register to controllers.
Diagnose
Check the device type in the wireless tray. Autonomous APs have their own SSID config; lightweight APs do not.
Fix
Replace it with a lightweight AP (LAP). Only lightweight APs join a WLC.
APs are joined but no SSID is broadcasting
Cause
The WLAN was created but not enabled, or no WLAN exists yet on the controller.
Diagnose
On the WLC GUI → WLANs: is CBT-Corp present and status Enabled?
Fix
Create the WLAN if missing, set its security, and toggle status to Enabled. It pushes to the joined APs automatically.
Client sees CBT-Corp but can't connect
Cause
Security mismatch — the client's pass phrase (or auth type) doesn't match the WLAN's security on the controller.
Diagnose
Compare the client's WPA2-PSK pass phrase to the WLAN security setting on the WLC.
Fix
Re-enter the matching pass phrase (CBTcorp2024) on the client, or correct the WLAN security on the controller.
Client drops its connection when moving between APs (roaming fails)
Cause
The two APs aren't broadcasting the same SSID/security, so moving between them looks like joining a different network. (With a WLC pushing one WLAN, both should match automatically — unless one AP failed to join.)
Diagnose
Confirm both APs are joined to the WLC and both broadcast the identical CBT-Corp WLAN.
Fix
Ensure both APs are registered to the controller so they share the one WLAN definition (same ESSID and security).
Can't reach the WLC's web GUI from a PC
Cause
The PC can't reach the management IP — wrong VLAN, no route, or the WLC management IP isn't set/active.
Diagnose
Ping 192.168.10.240 from the PC. Check the PC and WLC are both correctly addressed in VLAN 10.
Fix
Correct the addressing/VLAN so the PC can reach the controller, then browse to https://192.168.10.240.
Everything looks right but the AP shows "discovering" indefinitely
Cause
CAPWAP discovery hasn't completed — often just simulation timing in Packet Tracer, or the AP got its IP after the WLC was already up.
Diagnose
Verify the AP has an IP and can reach the WLC. Then advance simulation time.
Fix
Click fast-forward time a few times to let discovery/join complete. If it still fails, power-cycle the AP so it restarts discovery.
