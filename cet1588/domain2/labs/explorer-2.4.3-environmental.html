Keep the room alive.

Equipment doesn't just need power and a place to live \u2014 it needs the right air around it. Run a server room too hot and gear throttles and shuts down; too humid and you invite condensation and corrosion; too dry and static electricity starts zapping components. And when something does ignite, you'd better not be putting it out with water sprayed across live electronics. This explorer lets you drive a server room's temperature and humidity to see where the danger zones are, then compare the fire-suppression systems built for rooms full of computers.

The ranges worth memorizing

For the exam, hold onto the recommended band: roughly 64–81°F (18–27°C) for temperature and 40–60% relative humidity. You don't need exact numbers, but you must know the directions of harm: too hot = overheating, too dry = static/ESD, too humid = condensation and corrosion.

Why water is the last resort

Water conducts electricity and ruins circuit boards, so spraying it across powered racks can cause more damage than the fire. That's why electronics-heavy spaces prefer clean-agent gaseous suppression (no residue, safe for equipment), and where water systems are required, a pre-action design keeps the pipes dry until two separate triggers fire \u2014 minimizing the chance of an accidental soaking.

Key terms
Recommended temperature
Roughly 64–81°F (18–27°C). Too hot causes overheating and thermal shutdowns; too cold wastes energy and risks condensation on swings.
Relative humidity (RH)
Recommended ~40–60%. The percentage of moisture the air holds relative to its maximum at that temperature.
ESD
Electrostatic discharge — static buildup (worse in dry air) that can zap and damage sensitive components.
Condensation / corrosion
Risks of excessive humidity — moisture forms on surfaces, causing shorts and gradual corrosion of contacts.
Clean-agent (gaseous)
Fire suppression using inert gas or chemical agent that leaves no residue and is safe for electronics — the data-center preference.
Pre-action vs wet-pipe
Pre-action keeps sprinkler pipes dry until two triggers fire (safer near electronics); wet-pipe keeps them charged with water (standard buildings).
