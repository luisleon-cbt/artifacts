The complete reference.

Disaster recovery has four concept families that the exam tests heavily: metrics (RPO, RTO, MTTR, MTBF — and how they relate to availability), recovery sites (cold, warm, hot, active-active — what each can do and what each costs), backup strategies (full, incremental, differential, snapshots — the tradeoffs between backup speed, restore speed, and storage cost), and high availability (the architectures that prevent failures from causing downtime in the first place).

The Four Metrics
RPO

Recovery Point Objective

The maximum amount of data loss the business can tolerate, measured in time. RPO = 1 hour means "if we lose everything since the last backup, we can lose at most 1 hour of data."

DRIVES: Backup frequency. RPO 1h → back up at least every hour. RPO 5min → near-continuous replication. RPO 0 → synchronous replication.

RTO

Recovery Time Objective

The maximum downtime the business can tolerate after a failure, measured in time. RTO = 4 hours means "we must be back up within 4 hours of any disaster."

DRIVES: Site type. RTO 5 min → hot site. RTO 24h → warm site. RTO 1 week → cold site. RTO 0 → active-active.

MTTR

Mean Time To Repair

The average time to repair a failed component. Includes detection, diagnosis, parts acquisition, fix, and verification. Lower is better.

EXAMPLE: Vendor 4-hour MTTR means parts and tech arrive within 4 hours of opening a case. Affects RTO directly.

MTBF

Mean Time Between Failures

The average time a component runs before failing. Higher is better. Manufacturer estimate, typically 50,000-300,000 hours for server-grade hardware.

EXAMPLE: Hard drive rated MTBF 100,000 hours doesn't mean each drive lasts 100k hours — it means a population averages 1 failure per 100k drive-hours.

The availability formula links MTBF and MTTR: Availability = MTBF / (MTBF + MTTR). This is the math behind the "nines" of uptime — 99.9% means 43 minutes downtime per month, 99.99% means 4 minutes, 99.999% means 26 seconds. Each additional nine typically costs 10x more.

Backup Strategies
StrategyWhat it capturesBackup speedRestore speedStorage
Full Every file, every time Slow Fast Most
Incremental Only what changed since LAST backup (full or incr) Fast Slow Least
Differential Only what changed since LAST FULL Medium Medium Medium
Snapshots Point-in-time reference (block/file system level) Instant Instant Small + growth

The full-vs-incremental tradeoff: Incrementals are tiny and fast to take, but to restore Wednesday afternoon you need Sunday's full + Monday's incr + Tuesday's incr + Wednesday's incr — four files in sequence. Differentials are larger but only require Sunday's full + Wednesday's differential — two files. Snapshots are nearly-instantaneous reference points but typically depend on the underlying storage system continuing to function.

High Availability — Preventing Failures From Becoming Outages
REDUNDANCY

N+1, 2N, 2N+1

N+1: one extra component beyond what's needed (4 power supplies when 3 are required).
2N: full duplicate (two independent power feeds, two PSUs each).
2N+1: duplicate plus one spare.

LOAD BALANCING

Distribute traffic across many servers

A load balancer distributes connections across a pool of backend servers. When one fails, traffic flows to the rest. Eliminates single points of failure for application servers.

CLUSTERING

Multiple servers acting as one

Active/passive: one node serves, others wait to take over.
Active/active: all nodes serve simultaneously. Faster failover than single-server with backup.

NIC TEAMING

Redundant network connections

Multiple physical NICs bonded into a logical interface. Survives single NIC, cable, or switch port failure. Modes: active/passive failover, or load-balanced active/active (LACP).

DUAL POWER

Redundant power supplies

Enterprise servers have 2 PSUs, each fed from a different circuit (often A-side and B-side feeds from independent UPS systems). Survives a single PSU failure, a single circuit failure, or a single UPS failure.

GEOGRAPHIC REDUNDANCY

Multiple sites

Multiple data centers in different regions. Survives site-level disasters (fire, flood, regional power outage). Often combined with active-active or hot-site DR architectures.

Common Exam Traps
  • RPO drives backup frequency. RTO drives site type. They're related but not the same thing — a system can have a small RPO (frequent backups) and a large RTO (slow restoration acceptable) or vice versa.
  • Lower MTBF = LESS reliable. MTBF measures time between failures, so a higher number means longer intervals between failures = more reliable.
  • Lower MTTR = MORE recoverable. MTTR is repair time — shorter is better.
  • Cold site has SPACE only. No equipment in place. Recovery means shipping hardware, installing, restoring data — days to weeks.
  • Hot site has REAL-TIME data replication. If the data isn't replicating continuously, it's a warm site.
  • Incremental restore needs the full chain. Lose any one incremental backup file and the restore breaks. Differential needs only the most recent full + most recent differential.
  • Snapshots are NOT backups. A snapshot of a volume on the same array dies when the array dies. Backups must be on different media, preferably offsite.
  • The 3-2-1 backup rule: 3 copies of data, on 2 different media, with 1 offsite.
  • Active-active and hot site are different. Active-active means both sites serve traffic concurrently. Hot site means a standby site ready to take over — but it's not serving traffic until failover.

What this teaches

  • Business requirements drive technical architecture, not the reverse. The right starting question is "how much data loss and downtime can the business tolerate?" Not "what backup product should we buy?"
  • Cost grows exponentially. Going from a warm site to a hot site might be 2-3x cost. Going from hot to active-active is another 2-3x. Going from RTO 4h to RTO 1m might be 10x. Most organizations cannot afford zero RPO/RTO for everything — so they tier their applications by criticality.
  • RPO and RTO are independent. A document archive might have RPO 24h (lose a day of writes) but RTO 1 week (can be slow to restore). A trading system has RPO 0 AND RTO 0.
  • The exam loves matching scenarios to architectures. Practice mapping "this kind of business" → "this kind of site" → "this kind of backup." That mapping is what this selector trains.

The 3-2-1 rule

Industry standard for backup architecture: 3 copies of data, on 2 different media, with 1 offsite. Example: production data + on-prem backup appliance + cloud archive. Defends against single-drive failure, single-site disaster, and ransomware that encrypts all online storage. The modern variant is 3-2-1-1-0: add 1 immutable/offline copy and 0 errors in verification.

Common backup mistakes

  • Backups exist; restores have never been tested. Untested backups have a startling tendency to be unrestorable when you actually need them. Test restores quarterly at minimum.
  • Snapshot = backup. A snapshot is a point-in-time view on the same storage system. When the array dies, so do all the snapshots. Snapshots are great for "oops I deleted that file 10 minutes ago" — not for disaster recovery.
  • Backups all on the same network. Ransomware that encrypts production also encrypts network-reachable backups. The "1 offline" in 3-2-1-1-0 specifically defends against this — air-gapped or write-once media.
  • Backup retention is too short. Some malware sits dormant for weeks. If you only retain 7 days of backups, you'll restore the infection. Long retention (with tiered storage) matters.
